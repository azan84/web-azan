Back to Class as a Student: Day 1 of HRDC Train-the-Trainer

September 2026  ·  TTT series, part 1 of 4  ·  Crystal Crown Hotel

HRDC Train-the-Trainer · 4-part series

  3. Day 3 · Delivery, facilitation & evaluation

I have been standing in front of lecture halls for years. I know where the projector remote likes to hide, I know which row sleeps after lunch, and I can explain a Navier–Stokes term with one hand while fixing a dead HDMI cable with the other. So when I signed up for the HRD Corp Train-the-Trainer (TTT) programme, run by the Malaysian Institute of Management (MIM) from 21 to 25 September 2026, a small, arrogant voice in my head said: “How hard can this be? You teach for a living.”

That voice did not survive Monday morning.

Our trainer was Dr Raymond Lai Chong Teng, an engineer by training, a former MD, CEO and consultant, with 27 years of consulting and training behind him. He has been a MIM, HRDC and ATD master trainer for about two decades and has trained roughly 2,500 trainers. The first two days were at the Crystal Crown Hotel; the last three moved to MIM’s office in the PJ33 building, Seksyen 13. This is the first of four posts about the week. Days 1 to 3 covered the five TTT modules, and Days 4 and 5 were all mock presentations. Those get a post of their own, with photos, because they deserve one.

We Started by Singing. Yes, Really.

No PowerPoint about learning outcomes, no fire-exit briefing to open. Day 1 started with Negaraku and Generasi Muda – Aku Kasih Malaysia, sung by a room full of slightly confused professionals. Then came an emoji grid asking “How are you feeling now?” Happy, Excited, Anxious, Confident, Angry, Scared, Confused, Surprised. I would like to say I picked “Confident”. By Thursday, the honest answer was “Anxious”.

The point, we soon learned, was deliberate. Before any learning happens, a trainer has to get the room ready. The programme’s first summary slide put it in three neat pairs:

Lesson hook → Attention

Grab the brain before it wanders off to check WhatsApp.

Icebreaker → Warm-up

Get strangers talking, laughing, and moving.

Environment → Safety

Nobody learns when they are afraid to look silly.

The icebreaker was a round of brain teasers as an online quiz, played in subgroups. Nothing reveals someone’s true personality like a countdown timer and a leaderboard. The debrief question afterwards was simple: “How does this activity help in training?” I realised I had never once asked my students that question about anything I made them do.

The Scoreboard: How You Pass TTT

Early on, Dr Raymond laid out exactly how we would be judged. It was a refreshing dose of transparency, and a quiet warning about where the real weight sat:

Attitude10%
Knowledge (MCQ)30%
Skills: design + delivery60%

Passing score: 75%. Knowledge is a 45-question MCQ drawn from a 300-question bank. Skills means designing a course and delivering it in front of everyone.

Sixty percent on skills. In other words, you could memorise every model in the manual and still fail if you stood in front of the room and mumbled at your slides. Noted. Nervously.

The week followed five competency tasks, CT1 to CT5. Every module opened with that same roadmap, and by Friday I could draw it in my sleep:

  1. CT1 · Plan Adult LearningHow adults learn, and what that means for you as a trainer.
  2. CT2 · Conduct Training Needs Analysis (TNA)Find the gap before you build the training.
  3. CT3 · Design Competency-Based TrainingADDIE, ROPES, wow factors, objectives, slides. (Day 2, next post.)
  4. CT4 · Conduct Competency-Based TrainingDelivery, facilitation, questioning, difficult learners. (Part 3.)
  5. CT5 · Evaluate Training EffectivenessKirkpatrick’s levels plus Phillips’ ROI. (Also Part 3.)

“Why Do We Do What We Do?”: Your Brain on Training

The section I enjoyed most on Day 1 was called Valuable Insights, and it began with the brain. Information goes through a pipeline (the classic Atkinson–Shiffrin multi-store model):

~3 s

Sensory memory

A quick scan: is this important? If not, gone.

7 items

Short-term memory

Holds about seven things for 15–30 seconds. Then it leaks.

∞

Long-term memory

Store, recall, apply, possibly for a lifetime, if it gets there.

A cartoon of the multi-store model, not a lab result. The point: overflow the 7-slot buffer and skip rehearsal, and most of what you sent is gone.

As an engineer, I found this oddly satisfying. It is a filter cascade with a very small buffer in the middle. A typical lecture slide with 14 bullet points is basically a buffer-overflow attack on the audience. The manual turned this into a seven-step recipe for managing information processing:

  1. Make contents appealingThe brain decides in seconds whether to care.
  2. Capture attention: the “wow”A statistic, a story, a question, a video.
  3. Chunk informationRespect the seven-item buffer.
  4. Make connectionsAnalogies and examples hook new ideas onto old ones.
  5. RehearseRepeat, revisit, reuse.
  6. RetrieveQuizzes, and not just for marks. Recall strengthens the memory.
  7. ApplyUse it, or lose it.

Then came learning motivations. Extrinsic motivators (awards, praise, promotion, punishment) are what most of us lecturers lean on, because “this will come out in the exam” is the oldest trick in academia. The manual put far more weight on intrinsic ones: relevance, practicality, clarity, enjoyment, encouragement, safety, comfort, fairness and competence. When your design and delivery line up with those, learners form an emotional connection, and that connection carries them through attention, engagement, learning, retention and finally application.

Lecturer confession #1: I have spent years optimising my content. Dr Raymond spent the morning showing us that content is maybe half the battle. The other half is whether the person in the chair feels it is relevant, safe and worth their energy.

We also did a fun subgroup exercise called “The Best / The Worst”: describe the best and the worst trainer you have ever had, in terms of design and delivery. Every group’s “worst” list looked suspiciously alike: reads from slides, never asks questions, runs over time, tiny fonts. Several of us quietly avoided eye contact with each other.

Module 1: Adults Are Not Big Children

The key idea of Module 1 is the difference between pedagogy and andragogy:

The six adult learning principles made it concrete. Adults are self-directed, goal-oriented, need relevance, want things to be practical, bring experience to the room, and expect respect. If you have ever watched a senior engineer with 20 years of plant experience sit through a lecture that ignores all of that, you know exactly what disengagement looks like. Arms folded, phone out, eyes glazed.

Then we toured the classic learning theories:

TheoryThe idea in one line
Kolb’s cycleConcrete experience → reflective observation → abstract conceptualisation → active experimentation. Learning is a loop, not a lecture.
Thorndike’s lawsReadiness (hook them first), Exercise (practise) and Effect (positive feedback makes it stick).
Carl RogersCongruence: communicate honestly, acknowledge concerns, and admit when you don’t know. “I don’t have all the answers yet, but I can share what has helped me before.”
Fleming’s VAKVisual, Auditory, Kinesthetic. Most people lean visual (the manual cites ~65% visual, 30% auditory, 5% kinesthetic), but nobody uses only one style.
Honey & MumfordActivist, Theorist, Pragmatist, Reflector. Your room contains all four, whether you like it or not.

Kolb’s cycle, applied to a fluids lab

tap a stage

I laughed at the Carl Rogers line, because every lecturer has been asked a question in class they cannot answer. The two classic responses are to bluff confidently, or to say “good question, we’ll cover that later” and pray. Rogers suggests a third option, which is to just be honest. Apparently, adults respect that more than bluffing. Who knew.

Module 1 closed with the traits of a competent trainer: passion, energy, enthusiasm, adaptability, empathy and caring. It also covered the trainer’s skill styles (Instructor, Explorer, Thinker, Guide) and a code of ethics built on integrity, confidentiality, diversity, competence, accountability, fairness and professionalism. Then came a short online quiz to revisit the module, and the leaderboard made the room properly competitive.

Module 2: Training Needs Analysis, or “Measure Twice, Train Once”

The second module was where the engineer in me sat up straight. A Training Needs Analysis (TNA) is defined as a systematic process for finding the gap between actual and desired performance caused by a competency gap in ASK: Attitude, Skills, Knowledge. The most important question on the slide was: “Is training needed to fill the gap?”

That question matters because sometimes the honest answer is no. The manual lists non-training solutions that I wish more organisations knew about:

Process

Inefficient workflow? Redesign the process, don’t send people on a course.

Structure

Role confusion? Fix reporting lines or build cross-functional teams.

System

Bad technology? Upgrade or automate.

Environment

Poor lighting, heat or a cramped workspace? Fix the room.

Quick game: training, or not?

That is root-cause thinking, the same idea I wrote about in Stop Solving the Wrong Problem. Training is a solution, but it is not the solution to everything.

The TNA process itself runs in four steps: select an approach (survey, observation, interview, focus group, document review), gather data (from self, peers, superiors, subordinates), analyse, and design the solution. Data comes from three levels: the organisation (competency model), the department, and the individual (job description and task analysis). We worked with proficiency scales from 1 (Fundamental) to 5 (Expert), compared self-ratings against supervisor ratings, and read radar charts showing which staff level had the biggest gap.

The outputs are tangible: TNA findings, training and non-training solutions, a short-term and long-term training plan (a Gantt chart, the engineer’s comfort blanket), and a TNA report. The day closed with a quick quiz on our phones, a five-minute reflection, and the question that ended every module: “What actions will you take in your own training?”

What Day 1 Did to My Lecturer Brain

Walking out of Crystal Crown that evening, I kept thinking about my own classes. A university lecture is, mostly, pedagogy wearing andragogy’s clothes. We teach young adults, but we design for “cover the syllabus by Week 14”, not for “change what they can do on Monday morning”. My friends from the university, Dr Firdaus, Dr Dayana, Dr Wan and Dr Adibah, and I kept coming back to the same realisation: that the industry trainers around us were playing a completely different game.

Use this tomorrow

  1. Open with a hook, not a recapSpend the first two minutes making it relevant before teaching anything.
  2. Chunk to seven or fewerIf a slide has more than seven ideas, split it.
  3. Ask “is it really a training problem?”Before designing a course, check the process, system and environment first.
Content is what you know. Training is what they can do afterwards. Day 1 was about learning the difference.
