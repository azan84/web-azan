The One Number Data Centres Brag About

July 2026  ·  What “PUE” really measures

Every time you stream a film, send a voice note, or ask an AI a question, a building full of computers somewhere springs into action on your behalf. We call it a data centre — picture a warehouse packed wall-to-wall with servers, humming 24/7 and running hot. And here is the thing about hot computers: keeping them cool can cost nearly as much electricity as the computing itself.

So every data centre really pays two electricity bills at once:

Bill 1 · The actual work

The servers doing your computing — loading the page, running the model, storing your photos.

Bill 2 · The overhead

Everything that keeps those servers alive and cold — fans, pumps, chillers, power losses, lighting.

To grade themselves, the industry simply divides one bill by the other and calls the result PUE — Power Usage Effectiveness:

PUE  =  Total energy  ÷  Computing energy

A perfect score is 1.0: every watt goes to computing, nothing wasted on cooling. A PUE of 1.5 means that for every 2 units of useful computing, you burn 1 extra unit just keeping the room cold and the lights on. Lower is better — and everyone wants bragging rights to the smallest number.

Enter the servers’ bubble bath

One of the coolest (sorry) ways to chase a low PUE is immersion cooling: instead of blowing cold air over the servers, you literally dunk them in a special liquid that whisks heat away but doesn’t conduct electricity — a bit like giving your PC a bath in oil. The noisy fans disappear, and vendors love to advertise dazzling PUEs like 1.07.

The catch: one number, many meanings

Here is the puzzle my colleagues and I dug into. A PUE number, all by itself, can quietly fool you. In our study, a single test rig — same hardware, same climate, same textbook definition of PUE — produced numbers anywhere from…

1.057.81

Same rig, one unchanged definition — a sevenfold swing, just from how it was run and measured.

Nothing changed but the operating settings and exactly where you draw the line around “computing.” If one machine can score anywhere from wonderful to awful without changing the technology, then a bare number tells you surprisingly little.

It’s a bit like a car’s fuel-economy sticker. “20 km/L” sounds great — but was that city or highway, uphill or down, and whose test? Two data centres both boasting “PUE 1.1” might not be doing remotely the same thing.

So what should you ask?

Our review’s friendly takeaway: never trust a lone PUE number. Ask the four questions hiding behind it — where did they draw the boundary, what counted as computing, was it truly measured (or just simulated, or a vendor’s claim), and over what period? We even built a five-level scale for when two PUE figures can honestly be compared, plus a short checklist that makes any figure trustworthy.

A single efficiency score is a headline, not the whole story. So the next time you spot a shiny “PUE 1.1,” give it a friendly smile and ask: compared to what — and measured how?
