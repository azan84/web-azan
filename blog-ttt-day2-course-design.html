If Day 1 taught us how adults learn, Day 2 at the Crystal Crown Hotel taught us how to build something they will actually learn from. This was Module 3, Design Competency-Based Training, the thickest chunk of the manual. Halfway through it I realised that the slide decks I had been proudly reusing since my PhD days were, by TTT standards, crimes against typography.

The morning opened with a Padlet board and one question: “What makes a great training course design?” Everyone posted from their phones. The answers on the wall were good. The answers in our actual slide decks, as we were about to find out, were not.

ADDIE: The Engineering Design Process in Disguise

Competency-based training (CBT) is about equipping people with the specific competencies their role needs. The backbone is the ADDIE model, which the manual traces back to Florida State University and military training in the 1970s:

  1. AnalyseWho is the audience, what do they need, and where are the competency gaps? (Hello again, TNA.)
  2. DesignObjectives, methods, activities, resources.
  3. DevelopBuild the materials, pilot them, refine.
  4. ImplementFacilitate and engage.
  5. EvaluateDid it work?

As a mechanical engineer, I nearly applauded. This is the engineering design process with a different hat on: requirements, concept, prototype, deploy, test. I have taught that loop for years. I had just never applied it to my own lectures.

Alignment runs through all of it: overall goal → Bloom’s taxonomy → learning objectives → learning outcomes. Bloom’s pyramid (Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyse, Evaluate, Create) came back with a twist. Dr Raymond’s slide labelled the lower levels “Good” and the top, Create, “Great!”. The aim of the week was to go from good to great.

ROPES: The Skeleton of Every Good Session

The model that shaped everything we built that week was ROPES. Once you see it, you can’t unsee it, and every training session you attend afterwards gets silently graded:

R · ReviewGrab attention, warm upWow factor (lesson hook), icebreaker
O · OverviewSet directionLearning objectives, modules and submodules
P · PresentationDeliver the content clearly and logicallyMethods, resources, sequencing, roadmap, visuals, colour contrast, transitions
E · ExercisePractise and assessLearning activities, assessments
S · SummaryConsolidate for transferKey takeaways, reflection on objectives, putting it together, action plan

R: The Wow Factor (Where Everyone Got Competitive)

Every presentation should open with a wow factor, a lesson hook that makes the audience care in the first two minutes. The four basic ingredients are statistics, story, question, video, in any combination. What I didn’t know was that there is a structure underneath, built on a Think → Feel → Do model:

  1. The fact (Think)A statistic, story or video that changes what they believe. “Did you know complaints rose 50% and sales dropped 60%…”
  2. The provoking question (Feel)Something that stirs emotion. “Can we afford to let this go on?” or “What would it cost you if this goes wrong?”
  3. The motivating question (Do)Get their commitment. “Are you ready to learn how to prevent that?”

A wow can be framed as a threat (“this is costing us”) or an opportunity (“property prices rose 15% a year — are you excited to begin?”). In our subgroup exercise we wrote a hook for a Sales Techniques course from a chart where costs went up 50% and sales went down 50%. The slide added, in red: “create the provoking and motivating questions (very important)!” Ten minutes, and the room got loud.

Lecturer confession #2: My usual lecture opener is “Okay, last week we covered…”. That is not a wow factor. That is a sedative.

I rebuilt my own TTT presentation, on creating your first IoT idea, around a much stronger hook. More on that in part 4.

Icebreakers also got a proper taxonomy: just for fun (two truths and a lie, a blind draw), based on the training objectives (brain teasers, gamification), and introducing the topic (a group discussion on the subject itself). The best icebreakers, it turns out, secretly teach.

O: Objectives That Actually Mean Something

A learning objective is a specific, measurable action participants can perform after the training. TTT uses the ABCD format:

A

Audience

Who?

B

Behaviour

Will do what?

C

Condition

Given what?

D

Degree

How well? How often?

And then there was the blacklist. Banned verbs: aware, know, understand, learn, ensure. The slide’s verdict was “Too general!” I went quiet, because “Students will understand…” appears in more of my course outlines than I would like to admit. TTT prefers verbs you can observe. Knowledge: define, state, explain. Skills: adjust, operate, assemble. Attitude: listen, accept, choose.

Every objective slide also has to open with the same phrase: “Upon completion of this course, you will be able to:” You will see that exact sentence on the screen in several photos in part 4.

P: Roadmaps, Colours, and the Cart Before the Horse

Content sequencing got its own cartoon: a man pushing a cart in front of a horse, captioned “You are putting the cart before the horse!!” The rule is to move from general to specific, chunk the content, and make the logic obvious. Then show it as a roadmap (Module 1 → 2 → 3 on a winding road) and roll the modules out in exactly that order. The slide said “Always remember this!” and we did, because it was on the checklist.

On visuals, the manual is direct: more pictures, fewer bullets. Turn lists into diagrams and processes into flows. Never use red text on a black background, or the reverse. Animations should follow four principles: simplicity, sequence, suitability, consistency. In other words, the “bounce-in-with-sound-effect” transition is officially retired.

The full list also covers the wow factor, self-introduction, “learning comfort” rules, timekeeping, the icebreaker, learning methods, objectives, colour contrast, a conclusion, and a session plan placed after the Thank You slide. That night I reopened my own deck with the checklist beside it, and the red pen came out more often than I expected. The checklist doesn’t care that you have a PhD.

E and S: Do, Then Wrap It Up Properly

The Exercise stage came with a quote attributed to Confucius: “I hear and I forget, I see and I remember, I do and I understand.” The menu of activities is wide: quizzes, brainstorming, case studies, hands-on work, games, role play and group discussion. Online tools include Kahoot!, Mentimeter, Blooket, Padlet, Slido and Google Forms. Assessments split cleanly into knowledge (the what and why: quiz, Q&A, test, case study) and skills (the how: demonstration, role play, simulation, project).

The Summary is not just a “Thank you, any questions?” slide. It has four parts: key takeaways, reflection on the learning objectives, putting it all together on the roadmap, and an action plan with real dates. Then comes the session plan, which lists what is learnt, in what sequence, with what resources and methods, and how long each module takes. For a lecturer, it is basically a lesson plan, done properly.

The Gallery Walk

Module 3 ended with a gallery walk. Each team picked a lead and took one topic: training methods, training activities, the principles of animation, ABCD objectives, or the ROPES framework. For each one we had to answer three questions: What is it about? Why is it important? How can it be applied in training? Then the leads presented and everyone else wandered between the outputs to critique and steal ideas. Because it was our own course topics on the line, it turned into a free design review. My team's slides ended up full of IoT examples, which tells you exactly what I was rehearsing in my head all week.

Use this tomorrow

  1. Rewrite one objective in ABCDDelete “understand” and replace it with something you can observe.
  2. Draw the roadmap firstModules in order, then build the slides to follow it.
  3. Add one practical activityThe checklist calls it “A MUST” for a reason.
A great course is designed backwards, from what people must be able to do, and opened forwards, with a hook they can’t ignore.

That was the end of our two days at Crystal Crown. In part 3 we move to MIM’s office in PJ33 and learn to actually deliver: voice, body language, difficult learners, and how to prove a training programme made money.
